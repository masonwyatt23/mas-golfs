# Dunmore Links — Course Two

**Par 72 · 7,195 yards · Outer Banks, North Carolina.** A true seaside links on the Atlantic dunes, designed in the links school — the ground game is the game, the wind is the main defense, and the sea is always in view. Two loops of nine, each par 36, each with two par 3s, two par 5s, and five par 4s. Four par 3s, four par 5s, ten par 4s in total.

> Dunmore Links is Wyatt National's opposite on purpose. Wyatt is parkland: trees, water, an aerial game, angles through pines. Dunmore is links: dunes, fescue, pot bunkers, a burn, and a sea wind that changes the golf by the hour. Same structure discipline, completely different character — so the show never plays the same golf twice.

## The name and the place

**Dunmore** — an old Scots word for a fort on a hill, borrowed for the highest dune on the property, where the 17th tee and the 18th green sit. The course is fictional, but the land is real in spirit: a narrow strip of dune land on the Outer Banks, between the Atlantic and the sound, where the wind never stops and the ground is sand under a thin skin of fescue. No trees anywhere — nothing to stop the wind, nothing to frame a shot except the dunes themselves. The sea is the boundary on the east side of the property; the sound is the boundary on the west. The clubhouse sits in a hollow between the two, low and wind-bent, with the 1st tee and the 18th green sharing a double green in front of it (the St. Andrews touch).

## Design philosophy — the links school

**The ground game (Old Tom Morris).** On a links the ball is meant to run. Fairways are firm and fast, greens are firm and fast, and the correct shot is very often a low runner, a bump-and-run, or a putter from 30 yards off the green — not a high flop. The sim's bounce/roll model and the commentary's vocabulary both change here: distance is earned on the ground, and a shot that lands short can still be a good shot.

**The wind is the defense (the links' first principle).** There is no length on the card that the wind cannot double. Dunmore's par 72 plays 7,195 yards on a calm morning and effectively 7,800 into a gale — and the wind changes by the hour, not by the day. The routing runs in every compass direction so no two consecutive holes play the same wind, and the morning sea breeze (northeast) and the afternoon land breeze (southwest) make the same nine play differently before and after lunch. The broadcast's wind readout becomes a character: flags, dune grass, and the sea itself tell Mas the strength before he swings.

**Templates, different ones (C.B. Macdonald / Seth Raynor).** Wyatt National borrowed Redan, Eden, Cape, Short, and Road Hole. Dunmore borrows the other half of the template book: a **Biarritz** par 3 (No. 3), a **Punchbowl** par 3 (No. 12), a **Double Plateau** par 3 (No. 16), an **Alps** par 4 (No. 17), a **Principal's Nose** par 4 (No. 5), and a **Sahara**-flavored par 5 (No. 6). The sim treats each template as a known strategic pattern, same as Wyatt's — the two courses share the pattern library, not the patterns.

**Minimalism, harder (Doak, Coore & Crenshaw — and the land itself).** A links is found, not built. The dunes already had the holes in them; the routing follows the natural valleys between them, and the only earth moved was for tees, greens, and the burn's crossing. Fairways are wide — width is the welcome on a links, because the wind is the punishment. Bunkers are small, deep, revetted pot bunkers: one shot in, one shot out, a full stroke lost, never a heroic escape. They are placed to punish the wrong angle and the wrong wind, not the miss-hit.

**Blindness is a feature (the links exception to MacKenzie).** MacKenzie allows blindness on the tee shot, never on the approach. A links allows it in more places: the Alps 17th is blind off the tee *and* the approach is semi-blind over a dune ridge; the Sahara 6th hides the fairway behind a dune shoulder. The broadcast's job is to make blindness legible — the aerial flyover, the tracer, and the pin cam do the work the eye cannot do from the tee.

**No trees, no water hazards in the parkland sense.** The hazards are the burn (a narrow, reed-lined stream crossing Nos. 2 and 14), the sea (out of bounds and lost ball on the east side of Nos. 8, 10, and 18), the pot bunkers (over 110 of them), the fescue (knee-high, playable but punishing — a ball in it is a half-shot lost), and the dunes themselves (a ball in a dune hollow is a blind punch-out). Water is functional and rare: it guards the bold line on exactly two holes, never the safe one.

**Green complexes.** Links greens are the opposite of Wyatt's: they are open at the front, sloping *toward* the fairway at 1–2% so the running ball can feed on, with fall-offs at the back and sides and shaved collection hollows instead of rough. The safe miss is short — on the ground, running up. The penalty miss is long or above the hole, where the putt runs away toward the sea. Double green on 9 and 18: one vast putting surface shared by two holes, with a spine down the middle — the biggest green either course has.

## Front nine — out (par 36, 3,591 yards)

### 1. The First Light — Par 4, 398 yards, downwind along the dunes
- **The question:** Can you start with a runner, not a flyer?
- **Ideal line:** A low drive down the left side, 260–270 yards, letting the firm ground add 20 yards of run. The ball should land short of the crest and run through it — the first lesson of the course, taught on the first hole.
- **Wrong miss:** Right, into the fescue on the dune face. The lie is playable but the ball sits down; the approach must be a high soft one, which the wind will not allow.
- **Green complex:** Open at the front, sloping gently toward the fairway at 1.5%, with a collection hollow right. The double green is shared with the 18th — the pin on the 1st is on the left lobe, the 18th's on the right, separated by a spine. A putt from the wrong lobe is a three-putt waiting to happen.
- **Wind/elevation:** Downwind with the morning sea breeze; plays ~375. Flat, exposed — the dune grass shows the wind strength from the tee.
- **School:** Strategic, gentle. **Template:** none — a pure opener, links-style.

### 2. The Burn — Par 5, 562 yards, dogleg right, the burn crossing twice
- **The question:** Do you carry the burn twice, or play it as a three-shot hole?
- **Ideal line:** A drive up the left side, then a long second that carries the burn at 300 yards and runs on to 240 yards from the green. The bold line also opens the green, which is angled away from the safe route.
- **Wrong miss:** The burn, obviously — but the subtler miss is the lay-up that runs too far on the firm ground and trickles in. On a links, the lay-up has to be *short* of where you think, because the ball never stops.
- **Green complex:** Large, 34 yards wide, tilted toward the burn. A draw into the green holds; a fade runs toward the water. Front bunker is a deep pot — a lie you cannot spin from, and the only play is out sideways.
- **Wind/elevation:** Slightly downhill to the burn, then flat. Into the wind it is a three-shot hole for everyone; downwind the eagle putt is real. The reeds show the wind before Mas swings.
- **School:** Heroic. **Template:** none — the burn is the hole.

### 3. The Biarritz — Par 3, 178 yards, the swale
- **The question:** Can you carry the swale, or do you use it?
- **Ideal line:** A mid-iron that lands on the front plateau and runs through the 4-foot-deep swale to the back plateau — the Biarritz lesson: the ground is part of the shot. The safe play is front plateau, two putts; the bold play is the back pin, which demands the carry.
- **Wrong miss:** Short of the swale — the ball dies in the dip and the putt is 60 feet over a valley. Long — a pot bunker behind, from which the putt runs back through the swale.
- **Green complex:** A Biarritz green, 60 yards deep in total: front plateau, swale, back plateau. The longest green on either course. Pins on the back plateau are nearly unholdable by air; they are made by the ground.
- **Wind/elevation:** Flat, across the prevailing wind (left-to-right). The wind pushes the ball toward the right pot bunker — Mas must start it left and let the wind bring it back.
- **School:** Strategic. **Template:** Biarritz.

### 4. The Gauntlet — Par 4, 445 yards, straight into the wind
- **The question:** Can you hit two genuinely good shots into a sea wind?
- **Ideal line:** A low, piercing drive at the left-center pot bunker — carry it (250 yards) and the ball runs forward down the slope; lay up short and the approach is 200 yards uphill into the wind.
- **Wrong miss:** Right fescue. The fairway tilts right-to-left, and the right fescue is the thickest on the course; from it the green is blind behind a dune ridge.
- **Green complex:** The largest on the course (7,600 sq ft), three sections separated by a spine. A long approach on the wrong section leaves a 60-foot putt over the spine. The green slopes toward the fairway at 1.3% — it accepts a low runner and rejects a thin flyer.
- **Wind/elevation:** 25 feet uphill, straight into the prevailing wind. Plays 470+ on a still day, 500+ into a gale. This is the hole where Mas clubs up and hits the knockdown — and on a links, the knockdown is not a special shot, it is the default.
- **School:** Strategic, leaning penal. **Template:** none.

### 5. The Nose — Par 4, 372 yards, drivable, Principal's Nose
- **The question:** Driver at the green, or iron to the fat of the fairway?
- **Ideal line:** Driver, low and running, carrying the Principal's Nose — a cluster of three pot bunkers in the center of the fairway at 280 yards — and running on to the front apron. In a helping wind the green is reachable; into the wind it is not, and the correct play is a 5-iron to 120 yards.
- **Wrong miss:** The Nose itself — three deep pots from which the only play is out sideways. Or long, over a green that sits on a plateau with fall-offs on three sides.
- **Green complex:** Small (5,100 sq ft), firm, sloping toward the fairway — approaches must land short and run on; aerial attacks bounce through. A shaved run-off left invites the bump-and-run.
- **Wind/elevation:** Flat, but the most wind-exposed tee on the course — no trees, no dunes, so the wind is honest and readable. Downwind it is the easiest birdie on the course; into the wind it plays like a 400-yard par 4.
- **School:** Heroic. **Template:** Principal's Nose. **Inspiration:** the driveable par 4s of the great links — the decision, not the distance, is the test.

### 6. The Gales — Par 5, 585 yards, into the wind, the Sahara
- **The question:** Can you make three good shots when the wind is the hole?
- **Ideal line:** Driver, then a 3-wood to the left side of the fairway (avoiding the dune shoulder that hides the second landing area), then a mid-iron in. There is no shortcut — the hole is a test of execution and wind judgment, not decision.
- **Wrong miss:** The dune shoulder on the second shot — a blind drive over it that lands in the hollow is a punch-out. Or the right fescue off the tee, from which the second shot is blind over a ridge.
- **Green complex:** The most heavily defended on the course: pot bunkers front-left, front-right, and long, with a narrow entrance. The approach must be precise, not just long. It slopes toward the fairway at 1.3%, accepting a low runner.
- **Wind/elevation:** Flat, straight into the prevailing wind. Plays 620+ into a gale. The longest hole on the course, and the one where Mas's +3 shows — three good shots is par; anything less is a bogey.
- **School:** Penal, honestly so — the one hole where wind and execution are the test. **Template:** Sahara (the blind second-shot shoulder).

### 7. The Hollow — Par 3, 214 yards, over a dune hollow, into the wind
- **The question:** Can you commit to a long iron over a hollow when the only safe miss is short?
- **Ideal line:** A high hybrid or 4-iron, landing on the front third and releasing to the middle. The green is wide (36 yards) but shallow (24 yards deep) — distance control is everything, and the wind is the defense.
- **Wrong miss:** Long — a pot bunker behind, from which the putt runs back toward the hollow. Short — the dune hollow, a blind punch-out from a sandy lie. The safe miss is the front pot bunker, not the hollow.
- **Green complex:** Wide, shallow, with a deep pot bunker front-right and a hollow back-left. The putting surface slopes gently toward the hollow — putts break toward it, a visual deception the dunes provide for free.
- **Wind/elevation:** Flat, directly into the prevailing wind, fully exposed. Plays 235+ into a breeze. On a calm day it is a mid-iron; into the wind it is the hardest par 3 on the course.
- **School:** Heroic, softened by the front bunker as a save. **Template:** none.

### 8. The Shore — Par 4, 431 yards, dogleg left along the sea
- **The question:** Draw it around the corner, or play the long way around?
- **Ideal line:** A draw starting at the dune line and bending 25 yards, cutting the corner and leaving 95 yards in. The draw also matches the fairway's left-to-right tilt, so the ball runs forward on the firm ground.
- **Wrong miss:** Straight — through the fairway into the dunes, where the only play is a punch-out. Or over-drawn — into the left fescue, from which the green is blind behind the corner dune. The sea is out of bounds on the right for the whole hole; the wind pushes everything toward it.
- **Green complex:** Angled 30 degrees to the fairway, open on the left, guarded by a deep pot bunker on the right. The correct angle (from the corner) sees the whole green; the safe angle (from the right) sees only a sliver. The approach rewards the correct angle — MacKenzie's rule, on a links.
- **Wind/elevation:** Downhill 15 feet, then flat. The prevailing wind is across, right-to-left — it helps the draw and punishes the fade, and it pushes everything toward the sea.
- **School:** Strategic. **Template:** none.

### 9. The Return — Par 4, 406 yards, downwind to the clubhouse
- **The question:** Can you finish the nine with a scoring hole?
- **Ideal line:** Driver at the front-right pot bunker, fading it off the slope, leaving 110 yards in. The fairway is generous; the hole rewards a confident swing, and the firm ground adds 20 yards of run.
- **Wrong miss:** Left fescue, from which the double green — tucked behind that front-right bunker — is blind. Long is the clubhouse terrace; the gallery sees everything.
- **Green complex:** The double green, shared with the 1st: wide and shallow, sloping toward the fairway at 2%, with a false front. The front-right pot bunker is the only guard; the green is otherwise open, inviting the attack. The pin on the 9th is on the right lobe, the 1st's on the left.
- **Wind/elevation:** 20 feet downhill; plays ~385. Helping wind. The easiest approach on the front nine — a birdie hole that sets up the turn.
- **School:** Strategic, gentle. **Template:** none.

## Back nine — in (par 36, 3,604 yards)

### 10. The Dunes — Par 4, 424 yards, elevated tee, dogleg left
- **The question:** Can you hold the left side of a fairway that wants to feed everything right?
- **Ideal line:** A fade off the elevated tee, landing on the left half. The fairway tilts right-to-left on the landing area, so a draw runs into the right fescue; the fade holds the line.
- **Wrong miss:** Right fescue — thick, downhill lie, approach to a green that is open only from the left. The approach from the right sees a pot bunker and a narrow sliver. The sea is out of bounds on the right for the second half of the hole.
- **Green complex:** Long and narrow (38 yards deep, 20 wide), tilted toward the fairway, with a spine down the middle. Front pin is accessible; back pin requires carrying the spine. The green sits in a natural amphitheater of dunes, so the gallery (and the cameras) see the whole approach.
- **Wind/elevation:** 25 feet down from the tee, then flat. The elevated tee makes the wind readable — the flag is visible from the tee box, a MacKenzie visibility rule.
- **School:** Strategic. **Template:** none.

### 11. The Second Wind — Par 5, 578 yards, uphill into the wind, all the way
- **The question:** Can you make three genuinely good shots in a row, twice in one round?
- **Ideal line:** Driver, then a 3-wood to the left side of the fairway (avoiding the pot bunkers at 300 yards on the second shot), then a mid-iron in. There is no shortcut — the hole is a test of execution, not decision, and the wind makes it a different test than No. 6.
- **Wrong miss:** The fairway pot bunkers on the second shot — two deep, revetted pots that cost a full stroke. Or the right fescue off the tee, from which the second shot is blind over a ridge.
- **Green complex:** The second-most defended on the course: pot bunkers front-left, front-right, and long, with a narrow entrance. The green is large but the entrance is narrow — the approach must be precise, not just long.
- **Wind/elevation:** 40 feet uphill overall, straight into the prevailing wind. Plays 610+ into a breeze. The mirror of No. 6, from the other direction — the round's second examination.
- **School:** Penal, honestly so. **Template:** none.

### 12. The Punchbowl — Par 3, 165 yards, downhill into a bowl
- **The question:** Can you enjoy the prettiest hole on the course and still hit the shot?
- **Ideal line:** A smooth wedge, high and soft, to the middle of the bowl. The hole is short; the test is the mind, not the yardage — and the bowl forgives a slightly imperfect strike by feeding it toward the hole.
- **Wrong miss:** Short — the dune hollow in front, from which the punch-out must carry the bowl's lip. Long — a pot bunker behind, from which the putt runs back toward the bowl. The green is framed by dunes, and the beauty is the hazard: the view pulls the eye up and the swing with it.
- **Green complex:** A Punchbowl green: a sunken putting surface ringed by dunes, 28 yards wide, sloping gently toward the center. Two pin zones: front (the Sunday pin, near the lip) and back (safe, the everyday pin). The putting surface is the flattest on the course — the hole is won or lost on the tee shot.
- **Wind/elevation:** 20 feet downhill; plays ~150. Sheltered by the dunes, so the wind is light — but the dune grass shows every breath of it.
- **School:** Heroic, in miniature. **Template:** Punchbowl. **Inspiration:** the 17th at Sawgrass in spirit (water replaced by a bowl, nerves, a small target) at a friendlier scale.

### 13. The Anvil — Par 4, 452 yards, narrow, uphill, the hardest par 4
- **The question:** Can you hit the fairway and then hold a green that slopes away from you?
- **Ideal line:** A low, drawing drive between the pot bunkers (240 yards, both sides), then a long iron or hybrid to the front of the green. The approach must land short — the green slopes away, so anything that lands on the putting surface runs through.
- **Wrong miss:** Either fairway pot bunker — both are deep, and from them the green is unreachable in regulation. Long of the green is a collection hollow with a downhill lie.
- **Green complex:** The most severe on the course: a two-tier green sloping away from the fairway at 2%, with a false front and a run-off long. The only way to hold it is to land the approach on the front apron and let it run on — the ground game, Hanse-style, is the correct play.
- **Wind/elevation:** 35 feet uphill, into the wind. Plays 480+. The hardest par 4 on the course, and the stroke index 1.
- **School:** Penal, softened by the run-up option. **Template:** none.

### 14. The Dare — Par 4, 384 yards, the burn crossing at 280
- **The question:** Carry the burn or lay back?
- **Ideal line:** Driver over the burn (carry 285 yards) leaves a flip wedge in — but only if the wind is helping or calm. Into the wind, the correct play is a 5-iron to 130 yards, short of the burn.
- **Wrong miss:** The burn, obviously — but the subtler miss is the lay-up that runs through the firm fairway into the burn from the other side. The burn crosses diagonally, so the lay-up line matters as much as the carry, and the firm ground makes the lay-up run farther than it looks.
- **Green complex:** Small and firm, sloping toward the fairway, with a shaved run-off on the left. The approach from 60 yards must be a full, committed wedge — a tentative one spins back off the front.
- **Wind/elevation:** Flat. The burn makes the wind visible and the decision binary — the hole's drama is entirely in the tee-shot choice.
- **School:** Heroic. **Template:** none.

### 15. The Downwinder — Par 5, 548 yards, reachable in two downwind
- **The question:** Go for the green in two, or lay up to your number?
- **Ideal line:** Driver, then a high 3-wood or hybrid to the green — but only downwind. The green is guarded by a pot-bunker complex in front; the safe play is long and left, where a run-off feeds the ball back toward the hole.
- **Wrong miss:** The front pot-bunker complex — three deep pots that make up-and-down a 50/50 proposition. Short-right is the worst miss: a hollow from which the green is blind.
- **Green complex:** Large, 36 yards wide, with a back-left bowl that collects long approaches and feeds them toward the hole — the reward for the bold second shot. Front pins are guarded; back pins are accessible.
- **Wind/elevation:** Slightly downhill, usually downwind — the prevailing wind helps on this hole by design, so the eagle chance is real most days. Into the wind it becomes a three-shot hole, and the lay-up number (100 yards) is the play.
- **School:** Heroic. **Inspiration:** the second shot is the hole.

### 16. The Plateau — Par 3, 196 yards, two-tier green, deep front pot
- **The question:** Can you hit the correct tier from 200 yards?
- **Ideal line:** A mid-iron to the correct tier — front pin, land it short and let it release; back pin, carry it all the way to the top tier. The wrong tier is a three-putt waiting to happen.
- **Wrong miss:** The deep front pot bunker — 8 feet below the putting surface, with a revetted face. Or the wrong tier, which is a miss in everything but name.
- **Green complex:** A Double Plateau green: two flat tiers separated by a 3-foot step, tilted from front-right to back-left. The classic lesson applies — the shot that lands short-right and runs on is the correct play; the shot that flies at the flag is the sucker play.
- **Wind/elevation:** Flat, across the prevailing wind (left-to-right). The wind pushes the ball toward the pot bunker — Mas must start it left of the flag and let the wind bring it back.
- **School:** Strategic. **Template:** Double Plateau.

### 17. The Alps — Par 4, 439 yards, blind over the great dune, the highest point on the property
- **The question:** How much of the dune do you dare carry?
- **Ideal line:** A high fade over the great dune (the "Dunmore," the fort on the hill — 45 feet high), carrying 240 yards of sand and dune grass, landing on the far fairway and leaving 150 yards in. The fade also matches the out-of-bounds on the right — the shot shape and the hazard are in tension, which is the point. A marker post on the dune crest is the aiming line, as at Prestwick.
- **Wrong miss:** Short of the crest — the ball rolls back down the dune face into the valley, a blind punch-out. Or over the dune and right — out of bounds, stroke and distance, with the sea beyond it. The approach is semi-blind over a second dune ridge; only the flag is visible.
- **Green complex:** Narrow, 20 yards wide, guarded by a deep revetted pot bunker on the front-left, from which the only play is out sideways. A dune ridge lines the back of the green; anything long is a blind recovery from sand.
- **Wind/elevation:** 45 feet up to the crest, then down to the green. The tee shot is blind — the one fully blind tee shot on either course, earned by the hole's character. The wind at the crest is stronger than at the tee; Mas must read it from the dune grass on top.
- **School:** Heroic with a penal edge. **Template:** Alps. **Inspiration:** the 17th at Prestwick — the original Alps, where the blind shot is the point of the hole.

### 18. The Homecoming — Par 4, 418 yards, along the sea to a clubhouse green
- **The question:** Can you finish in front of everyone, with the sea at your back?
- **Ideal line:** Driver at the fairway pot bunker at 300 yards — carry it and the ball runs forward down the slope on the firm ground; lay up short and the approach is 170 uphill into the sea breeze.
- **Wrong miss:** The fairway pot bunker (deep, revetted, at driving distance — the last test). Or long of the green, where the gallery and the terrace see the recovery. The sea is out of bounds on the right for the whole hole.
- **Green complex:** The double green, shared with the 1st: wide and welcoming, sloping toward the fairway at 2%, with a false front and a back tier. The largest green on the back nine — a fitting stage. Front pins are birdie looks; back pins demand the approach land short and release.
- **Wind/elevation:** 20 feet uphill to a clubhouse green, with the gallery behind it. Helping wind off the sea. The approach is hit toward the people — the broadcast's closing shot, with the Atlantic behind the cameras.
- **School:** Strategic. **Template:** none.

## Routing notes

- **Two loops of nine** (MacKenzie principle 1): both nines start and end at the clubhouse. No long walks between green and tee.
- **Wind rotation:** holes run in every compass direction; no two consecutive holes play the same wind. The morning sea breeze (northeast) helps on 1, 5, 9, 15, and 18; hurts on 4, 6, 11, and 13; crosses on 3, 8, 10, 16, and 17. The afternoon land breeze (southwest) flips several of these — the same nine plays differently after lunch, which is a broadcast feature, not a problem.
- **Elevation rhythm:** the course is a dune walk, not a climb — low hollows and high crests in waves. The highest point is the 17th tee (the Dunmore, 45 feet); the lowest is the burn crossing on the 2nd. No hill climbing for its own sake: every rise is a strategic feature (a blind crest, a run-off, a wind read).
- **Difficulty arc:** the front nine opens gently and hardens (4, 6, 7); the back nine opens with a test (10, 11), breathes at the signature par 3 (12), grinds (13), gambles (14, 15), tests (16, 17), and closes with a scoring chance (18). The stroke index order: 13, 4, 6, 11, 17, 8, 2, 16, 10, 14, 7, 1, 15, 3, 5, 9, 18, 12.
- **Hazards:** the burn crosses 2 and 14; the sea is out of bounds on 8, 10, and 18; over 110 pot bunkers across the course; fescue on every hole; dunes on 8, 10, 17, and 18. Water is functional and rare — it guards the bold line on exactly two holes, never the safe one. The wind is the real hazard, and it is in play on all eighteen.

## Dunmore vs. Wyatt National — the contrast

| | Wyatt National (Course 1) | Dunmore Links (Course 2) |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | Blue Ridge foothills, Staunton, VA | Atlantic dunes, Outer Banks, NC |
| **Character** | Parkland — trees, lakes, creeks | Links — dunes, fescue, sea, burn |
| **The defense** | Angles through pines; water on the bold line | Wind; firm ground; pot bunkers |
| **The game** | Aerial — carry it, stop it, spin it | Ground — run it, bounce it, putt it |
| **The miss** | Long, above the hole | Long, above the hole — and into fescue |
| **Templates** | Redan, Eden, Cape, Short, Road Hole | Biarritz, Punchbowl, Double Plateau, Alps, Principal's Nose, Sahara |
| **Blindness** | One blind tee shot (17th) | Blind tee shots (6, 17), semi-blind approaches (17) |
| **Green defense** | Slope away from the fairway; deep bunkers | Pot bunkers; fall-offs; the wind |
| **Signature hole** | 12th, The Picture — pond, pines | 17th, The Alps — the great dune |
| **Scoring** | ~71–73 for a +3; 59 needs a near-perfect day | ~71–73 for a +3; 59 needs a near-perfect day *and* a calm wind |
| **The look** | Green, gold, blue ridges, pine-lined | Sand, fescue gold, grey-green sea, open sky |

The two courses are designed so that no skill transfers cleanly between them: a player who dominates Wyatt by carrying bunkers and spinning wedges must learn to keep the ball low, use the ground, and read the wind at Dunmore — and vice versa. Mas's learning loop (one fix per round) now has two curricula, and the broadcast can teach both.

## What a second course changes about the show

- **Course rotation.** The show alternates: Wyatt National one week, Dunmore Links the next (or one course per "season" of ~30 days, with a switch announced like a tournament venue change). Rotation is itself a story — the first round back at a course after a month away, the form that does or doesn't travel, the records that stand.
- **Two Road to 59 chases.** Each course gets its own 59 Watch, its own pace line, and its own "closest to 59" board. A 59 at Wyatt and a 59 at Dunmore are different achievements, and the second one is the bigger story. The ultimate chase is the **Dunmore–Wyatt Double**: a 59 on both courses. Nobody has done it, because nobody has played both — the show invents the record and then chases it.
- **Per-course records.** Course records, per-course scoring averages, per-course handicap differentials, per-course GIR/fairways/putts. The leaderboard splits by course and combines for the all-time board. A viewer can follow "Mas at Dunmore" as a separate thread from "Mas at Wyatt."
- **Two clip palettes.** Wyatt clips are green, gold, pines, water, the tracer against the ridges. Dunmore clips are sand, fescue, sea, sky, the ball running on the ground. The 60-day content calendar gets a second half — links weeks, wind weeks, ground-game weeks — and the formats that work at Dunmore (the knockdown, the bump-and-run, the blind Alps carry) are different from the ones that work at Wyatt.
- **Guest twins, twice over.** A guest's digital twin plays both courses against Mas. The guest who beats Mas at Wyatt may lose at Dunmore — the two-course format is a better test, and a better show.
- **Coins, per course.** Prediction markets are course-specific: the odds model already takes hole difficulty and conditions as inputs, so Dunmore's wind and firmness flow through the same engine. New market types appear naturally: "59 Watch at Dunmore," "first eagle at the Alps," "Mas's Dunmore scoring average this week."
- **The season structure.** A "season" becomes a two-course arc: the Wyatt leg, the Dunmore leg, and the Double. The 90-day launch playbook's second 60-day cycle is the Dunmore launch — a second opening day, a second premiere, a second wave of clips.

## What it takes to build

### In the web build

- **New hole data.** A second course JSON in the same schema as Wyatt's: 18 holes, pars, yardages, dogleg centrelines, green positions, bunker/pot positions, the burn polyline, the double green. The sim already reads course data generically — Dunmore is a data file, not a code change.
- **New terrain system.** Dune heightfield (smooth, rolling, wind-shaped — not Wyatt's ridge terrain), fescue texture (gold-green, patchy), sand texture for the pot bunkers and the dune faces, no trees (the tree system is simply not used), a sea plane on the east edge with animated water, and the burn as a narrow water strip. The terrain cache and shared-asset registry from the performance audit apply unchanged.
- **New lighting and sky.** Coastal light: brighter, harder, more open — a bigger sky, fewer clouds, a low sun that throws long shadows across the dunes. The same ACES grading, a different grade.
- **Sim tuning, per course.** The physics engine takes course parameters: firmer ground (more roll-out, longer drives, less spin), faster greens (higher stimp, more break), stronger and gustier wind (the wind model gains a gust component), pot-bunker lie rules (one shot out, no spin), and fescue lie rules (half-shot penalty, no shaping). The decision engine gains ground-game candidates: bump-and-run, putter from off the green, low runner — and the knockdown becomes the default into the wind, not the exception.
- **Broadcast changes.** New camera positions (the sea-side dolly on 8, the crest cam on 17), new hole flyovers, new lower-third templates ("DUNMORE LINKS · HOLE 12"), a second course card in the leaderboard, and the 59 Watch per course. The director state machine is unchanged — it reads the same event bus.

### In Unreal

- **New LiDAR.** USGS 3DEP coastal dune DEMs (1 m) for an analogous Outer Banks landform, same pipeline as Wyatt's slice: DEM → heightmap → Landscape, Gaea masks for dune/fescue/sand.
- **New Megascans.** Sand, dune grass, fescue, and coastal rock sets (free since Nov 5, 2026) — a different asset palette from Wyatt's pine-and-ridge sets. The ocean is a water material with a shoreline, same system as Wyatt's ponds, scaled up.
- **New MetaHuman wardrobe.** A windproof layer for Mas (same navy polo, plus a wind vest in the brand colors) and a cap that stays on in a gale — a small, visible detail the broadcast can joke about.
- **New cameras.** The sea-side dolly and the crest cam on the Alps, plus a drone shot that shows the whole dune strip between the Atlantic and the sound — the establishing shot the web build cannot do.
- **Cost.** Same as Wyatt's slice: the only purchase is Ultra Dynamic Sky ($49.99, already bought once — it is a per-project license, so it carries over). No new paid software. The two courses share the Unreal project; Dunmore is a second level, not a second build.
